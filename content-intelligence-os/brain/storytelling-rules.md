# Storytelling Rules

Structural rules for scripts. These apply to any narrative brand; documentary-specific format details live in `brands/<brand>/video-format.md`.

## The Non-Negotiables

1. **Open a loop in the first 15 seconds.** State or imply a question the viewer needs answered. Not "today we'll look at Nokia" but "In 2007, Nokia owned half the phones on Earth. Five years later it was worthless. The decision that killed it happened in a single meeting."
2. **Every scene must change something.** A stake rises, a belief flips, new information reframes what came before. If a paragraph could be deleted without the viewer noticing, delete it.
3. **Chronology is a tool, not a law.** Start at the moment of maximum tension (the collapse, the confrontation, the record high before the fall), then rewind. Pure birth-to-death chronology is the most common retention killer in documentary content.
4. **Concrete beats abstract, always.** "They were losing money" → "They were burning $4 million a day." "The CEO was under pressure" → "The board gave him 90 days." Numbers, dates, named people, physical details.
5. **One protagonist per act.** Even company stories are human stories. The viewer follows a person making decisions under pressure, not an org chart.

## Structural Template: The Loop Ladder

This is a brand-agnostic craft model for tension and loop mechanics — not an episode format. The canonical Empire Laws episode structure is the 9-section format in `brands/empire-laws/video-format.md`, which is what every Empire Laws episode must follow; the Loop Ladder below simply describes the loop-and-payoff craft those sections apply.

A retained video is a ladder of open loops, each closing as the next opens:

- **0:00–0:30 — Hook**: The biggest loop. The paradox or question the entire video answers.
- **0:30–2:00 — Stakes**: Why this matters, established through scale and consequence. End with a smaller loop ("But to understand the collapse, you need to see what they built first").
- **Act 1 — Rise**: Build investment in the protagonist. Plant the seed of the flaw *while the audience admires the success*. This is the most-skipped craft move and the most valuable one.
- **Midpoint Turn**: The moment the strength becomes the weakness. This should land near the middle of the video and be signposted as a turn.
- **Act 2 — Unraveling**: Consequences compound. Each event should feel both surprising and, in hindsight, inevitable.
- **Climax**: The single scene the whole video was built around. Slow down here — more detail, more tension, not less.
- **Resolution + Transfer**: What it means beyond this story. For lesson-driven brands, this is where the law gets named and made portable.

## Tension Mechanics

- **Dramatic irony**: The audience knows the iceberg is coming; the characters don't. Use forward-references sparingly ("This decision would look very different in three years").
- **The ticking clock**: Deadlines, runway, expiring patents, launch dates. If the real story has a clock, foreground it.
- **The worthy opponent**: A rival, a regulator, a market shift. Conflict without an antagonist force reads as a lecture.

## Language Rules

- Sentences under 20 words dominate. Long sentences are permitted only when the rhythm demands them.
- Active voice. "Kodak buried the digital camera," not "the digital camera was shelved."
- No throat-clearing: ban "In this video we're going to", "Before we start", "Without further ado", "Let's dive in."
- Rhetorical questions max twice per script. More than that reads as filler.
- Every statistic gets a comparison anchor: "$47 billion — more than the GDP of Croatia."

## The Delete Test

Before a script is approved, run each section through: "If I cut this, does the viewer's understanding of the climax weaken?" If no — cut it. Documentary scripts almost always improve by losing 15% of their length.
