# Example Scorecard Batch — Empire Laws

A worked example of the Idea Scoring workflow (`workflows/idea-scoring-workflow.md`) applying the Viral Predictor (`agents/04-viral-predictor.md`) and the 100-point Opportunity Scoring System (`brain/opportunity-scoring-system.md`) to ten candidate ideas.

This is a demonstration of the decision layer, not a production commitment. Scores here are illustrative — real scorecards cite live outlier data, current search results, and the actual competing thumbnails. **No score promises views. The framework improves the bet; the feed decides the outcome.**

All ten ideas pass the hard gates (none violate `forbidden-patterns.md`; all are strategic business stories with a transferable law). They are scored, then ranked, then the top three are chosen for production.

Scoring key: Curiosity Gap /20 · Market Proof /15 · Novel Angle /15 · Emotional Tension /15 · Audience Fit /10 · Thumbnail /10 · Title /10 · Feasibility /5 = /100.

## 1. Nokia heard the future and ignored it

| Dim | Score | Justification |
|-----|-------|---------------|
| Curiosity Gap | 17/20 | "Heard the future" implies a specific, knowable moment they were warned — the viewer needs to know what and when. |
| Market Proof | 13/15 | Nokia's collapse is a proven, repeatedly-covered topic across tiers; demand is real, competition is present. |
| Novel Angle | 12/15 | The "they were warned and ignored it" frame is sharper than generic "rise and fall of Nokia." |
| Emotional Tension | 13/15 | Tragic hubris — a giant that saw its own death coming and walked into it anyway. |
| Audience Fit | 9/10 | Bullseye: strategy, foresight, organizational denial — directly usable by operators. |
| Thumbnail | 8/10 | A cracking Nokia phone against a rising smartphone silhouette; strong single image. |
| Title | 9/10 | Multiple honest single-promise titles: "The Warning Nokia Ignored," "Nokia Saw the iPhone Coming." |
| Feasibility | 5/5 | Public research, chartable market-share collapse, archival imagery. No filming. |
| **FINAL** | **86/100** | |

- **Decision:** STRONG — REVISE FIRST (80–89)
- **Why it may work:** The tragedy of foresight is irresistible — a market leader that knew and did nothing is a story about denial every operator secretly fears in themselves.
- **Why it may fail:** Nokia is well-covered; if our angle collapses into "here's how Nokia declined," we're the tenth video and we lose on recognition.
- **Strongest hook:** "In 2004, a Nokia engineer demoed a touchscreen phone with an app store. Management killed it. Three years later, Apple shipped exactly that."
- **Best title direction:** Fatal-Decision pattern — "The Phone Nokia Killed Before Apple Built It."
- **Best thumbnail direction:** Moment-before-disaster — a Nokia phone mid-crack, faint smartphone rising behind it, one word: "IGNORED."
- **Main retention risk:** The middle sags if it becomes a corporate timeline. Keep it on the human decision-makers who said no.
- **How to improve:** Lock the angle to the SINGLE suppressed-innovation decision (not the whole decline) so the title promise and the climax are the same beat. That lifts Novel Angle and protects retention.
- **Produce / revise / reject:** REVISE — narrow to the one fatal decision, then produce.

## 2. Toys R Us was killed by debt

| Dim | Score | Justification |
|-----|-------|---------------|
| Curiosity Gap | 15/20 | We know it died; the live gap is HOW debt specifically did it — a mechanism, not an outcome. |
| Market Proof | 14/15 | Retail-collapse stories are among the most proven in the business-doc space. |
| Novel Angle | 12/15 | The leveraged-buyout-as-murder-weapon frame is sharper than "why Toys R Us closed." |
| Emotional Tension | 13/15 | Betrayal — a healthy company loaded with debt by its own owners and bled out. |
| Audience Fit | 9/10 | Directly relevant to founders and investors: what debt does to flexibility. Clean law. |
| Thumbnail | 8/10 | A shuttered toy store / Geoffrey the Giraffe as a gravestone motif; strong. |
| Title | 8/10 | "The Buyout That Killed Toys R Us"; single promise, honest. |
| Feasibility | 5/5 | Public filings, chartable debt load, stock/archival imagery. |
| **FINAL** | **84/100** | |

- **Decision:** STRONG — REVISE FIRST (80–89)
- **Why it may work:** "Killed by its own owners" reframes a boring bankruptcy into a betrayal thriller, and the law (debt kills flexibility) is one every founder feels.
- **Why it may fail:** If the debt mechanics are explained like a finance lecture, tension drains and the middle collapses.
- **Strongest hook:** "Toys R Us was profitable the year it died. It didn't run out of customers. It ran out of room to breathe under $5 billion in debt someone else put on its books."
- **Best title direction:** Fatal-Decision / Hidden-Cause — "The Deal That Secretly Killed Toys R Us."
- **Best thumbnail direction:** Before/after collision — bright toy store vs. the same store dark and empty, red debt figure across it.
- **Main retention risk:** The LBO explanation section. Anchor every number to a human consequence (jobs, stores, Christmas) or lose viewers there.
- **How to improve:** Personify the buyout — name the firms and the decision — so betrayal carries the finance. That protects Emotional Tension through the mechanism section.
- **Produce / revise / reject:** REVISE — humanize the debt story, then produce.

## 3. WeWork sold a dream, not a business

| Dim | Score | Justification |
|-----|-------|---------------|
| Curiosity Gap | 15/20 | "Sold a dream, not a business" promises the anatomy of a delusion — how did smart money buy it? |
| Market Proof | 14/15 | WeWork/Neumann is heavily proven; the collapse cycle drew huge audiences. |
| Novel Angle | 10/15 | Very heavily covered; our differentiation has to be the LAW, not the story. |
| Emotional Tension | 14/15 | Arrogance, delusion, a $47B valuation to near-zero — maximum charge. |
| Audience Fit | 9/10 | Perfect for founders/investors: narrative vs. fundamentals. |
| Thumbnail | 8/10 | Neumann's face + a collapsing valuation number; legible, tense. |
| Title | 8/10 | "How WeWork Sold a $47B Dream" — honest, specific, single promise. |
| Feasibility | 5/5 | Extensive public reporting, chartable valuation crash. |
| **FINAL** | **83/100** | |

- **Decision:** STRONG — REVISE FIRST (80–89)
- **Why it may work:** The gap between story and substance is the exact lesson our audience wants, and the collapse gives relentless tension.
- **Why it may fail:** Saturation. If we retell the same Neumann highlight reel everyone's seen, recognition works against us — the incumbents got there first and bigger.
- **Strongest hook:** "WeWork leased buildings long and rented desks short. That's not a tech company — that's a landlord with worse math. So why did the smartest investors on earth value it like Google?"
- **Best title direction:** Paradox — "The Business That Was Never a Business."
- **Best thumbnail direction:** Scale shock — a tiny desk beside a towering "$47B → $0" figure, Neumann small in frame.
- **Main retention risk:** Viewers who "already know the WeWork story" bounce early. The cold open must promise the MECHANISM (the landlord math), not the personality.
- **How to improve:** Anchor the whole video to the structural con (long leases, short revenue) rather than Neumann's antics — that's the un-saturated angle and it raises Novel Angle.
- **Produce / revise / reject:** REVISE — lead with the business-model lie, not the founder, then produce.

| Thumbnail | 6/10 | "Chaos" is abstract; best bet is Kalanick's face — image doesn't self-suggest. |

| Feasibility | 5/5 | Heavily documented publicly; chartable growth/burn. |
| **FINAL** | **79/100** | |

- **Decision:** CONDITIONAL (70–79)
- **Why it may work:** A founder who weaponized chaos and lost his company is a strong morality-of-growth story with a clear law.

## Production Decision — Top 3 Chosen

Nothing scored 90+, so nothing is an auto-produce. The three highest ideas are all strong candidates in the 80–89 band — produce them AFTER applying their prescribed fix, in this order:

1. **Nokia heard the future and ignored it (86)** — Pillar 1 (Collapse). Produce first: strongest curiosity gap in the batch, cleanest tragic arc, and a title/thumbnail that write themselves. Fix before production: lock the angle to the one suppressed-innovation decision so the promise and the climax are the same beat. Candidate law: *Ignoring the future is a choice, and the choice compounds.*

2. **Toys R Us was killed by debt (84)** — Pillar 1 (Collapse). Produce second: a betrayal thriller with a razor-clean, universally-felt law. Fix before production: name and personify the buyout firms so the finance carries emotional weight. Candidate law: *Debt kills flexibility.*

3. **WeWork sold a dream, not a business (83)** — Pillar 1 (Collapse). Produce third, with the most aggressive revision: the topic is saturated, so it only wins on the un-told angle. Fix before production: build the entire video on the structural con (long leases, short revenue) rather than Neumann's personality. Candidate law: *A story is not a business model.*

**Portfolio note:** all three top picks are Pillar 1 (Collapse). That is correct for a cold-start channel — collapse stories carry the highest proven demand and tension (per `brands/empire-laws/content-pillars.md` and the cold-start sequence in `brain/content-strategy.md`). But the next batch should deliberately surface a strong Pillar 2/3/4 candidate so the channel does not over-index on a single format. Airbnb (Rise, 81) is the leading non-collapse option and should be reworked toward the top of the following slot.

**What the framework did here:** it refused to hand out a single 90, forced every top idea to name and fix its weakest link before earning a production slot, and blunted five ideas into "conditional" or lower with concrete reasons — exactly the discipline that keeps a new channel from spending weeks producing a 72.
