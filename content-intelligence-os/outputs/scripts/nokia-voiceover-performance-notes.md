# Voiceover Performance Notes — "Nokia Heard the Future and Ignored It"

Delivery direction for the Nokia narration (`outputs/scripts/nokia-voiceover-script.md`), under the house rules in `brain/voiceover-system.md`. These notes shape *how* the verified words are performed. They never change the words.

- **Slug:** nokia-heard-the-future
- **Runtime target:** ~13–14 minutes at documentary pace (~1,830 words)

---

## Overall Voice Style

A strategist telling you something few people understand — calm, lower-register, close-mic, certain about what is known and honest about what isn't. Grave, not mournful. Intelligent, not academic. The listener should feel spoken *to*, one-on-one, not addressed by a broadcaster. Authority comes from restraint: the facts are dramatic on their own, so the voice stays composed and lets them land.

Reference feel: prestige streaming documentary narration. Never trailer-voice, never hype, never a smile in the sound.

## Pacing Guidance

- Base pace ~145–155 words per minute of active speech — measured, with real air between thoughts.
- **Speed up slightly** through setup and context (the middle of Section 2, the list-like passages in Section 4).
- **Slow down** through every reveal and turn: the cold-open contradiction, "it was speed" (Section 5), "Then Nokia jumped" (Section 6), and the whole Empire Law (Section 7).
- Let numbers and names sit — a fractional beat before and after "forty percent," "seven point two billion," "the N9."
- Never race the ends of sections; each section's final line is a small landing.

## Emotional Arc

The episode moves through five emotional registers — follow it, don't flatten it:

1. **Intrigue** (Section 1) — quiet, almost conspiratorial. A puzzle laid on the table.
2. **Grandeur, then unease** (Sections 2–3) — respect for the empire, shadowed by the planted seed.
3. **Tension** (Sections 4–5) — the ground shifting; the growing dread of a company that can see and can't move. This is the emotional climb.
4. **Gravity** (Section 6) — the collapse. Grave and steady, never gleeful. The N9 line carries genuine regret.
5. **Reflection, then resolve** (Sections 7–9) — the law delivered plainly; the modern warning turned toward the viewer; the final line quiet and certain.

The peak of *feeling* is the Collapse Point; the peak of *stillness* is the Law Card.

## Section-by-Section Delivery Notes

- **1. Cold Open** — Start almost hushed [calm][lower voice]. This is a secret. The three fragments ("Touchscreens." / "Phones that could reach the internet.") are placed, not rushed. Let the final question ("how did they still lose everything?") hang on a long pause — it is the hook that carries the whole video.
- **2. The Empire at the Top** — Confident and expansive, but not boastful. "Dominating" gets weight, not volume. Deliver "around forty percent" evenly and plainly — it is a sourced, scoped figure, not a boast. End ("the shadow was already there") drops lower — the first shadow of dread.
- **3. The First Crack** — [serious]. This is the pivot into tragedy. "A prototype is not a platform" is a key line — slight slow, clear weight. Keep it human, never mocking; Nokia is smart here, just hesitant.
- **4. The Enemy Changes the Game** — Measured and clear. The iPhone/Android passages are *informational*, delivered peripherally — do NOT let excitement about Apple or Google leak in; they are weather. Land firmly on "Knowing was never the problem," then a long pause — this is the door into the heart of the film.
- **5. The Internal Failure** — The core. [serious][lower voice]. Deliver the "researchers argued" line with the attribution audible — this is analysis, not verdict. Build slowly through fear, technical debt, and slowness. The final three lines ("Nokia's problem was never vision." / [slower] "It was speed.") are the intellectual climax — slow, low, certain, then a long pause.
- **6. The Collapse Point** — [tense]. The most eventful section. "Then Nokia jumped" lands hard, then a long pause. Deliver the two Microsoft figures *quietly* and precisely — scale speaks for itself; never punch them. The N9 lines carry regret, not scorn. "Effectively, gone" is near-whispered, final.
- **7. The Empire Law** — [serious][slower]. Step back from the story into plain truth. "Vision is cheap" is flat and certain. The Law Card line — "Hearing the future means nothing if your organization is too slow to act" — is the least-performed line in the episode: low, slow, almost still, framed by long pauses. Do not sell it.
- **8. Modern Warning** — Lift slightly back to measured directness. This is addressed to the viewer's own world. "How long it takes your company to get from knowing to doing" is the line that should make someone think of their own job — deliver it as a genuine question, not an accusation.
- **9. Final Line** — [lower voice][slower]. Three short lines, maximum stillness. "And time doesn't wait for giants" is quiet and final — resolve, not drama. Let the last pause breathe before the end screen.

## Words / Phrases to Emphasize (lean in — weight and slight slowing, never volume)

- Section 1: "**never turned them into one decisive product**"; "**saw it early**"; "**lose everything**"
- Section 2: "**dominating**"; "**machine**"; "one kind of **war**"; "**already there**"
- Section 3: "**already inside the building**"; "a **prototype is not a platform**"; "**couldn't commit to getting there first**"
- Section 4: "what customers expected a phone to **feel like**"; "the **rules changed**"; "the **wrong castle**"; "**Knowing was never the problem**"
- Section 5: "it was **slow**"; "**softened on the way up**"; "It was **speed**"
- Section 6: "Then Nokia **jumped**"; "They **fell hard**"; "**cut away its own foundation**"; "**gone**"
- Section 7: "**speed between knowing and doing**"; "**too slow to act**"
- Section 8: (none hard — keep it level and direct)
- Section 9: "**time doesn't wait for giants**"

One emphasis per sentence at most. If it starts to feel like selling, pull the emphasis back.

## Places Where Silence Matters (the long pauses do the work)

- After the cold-open question ("how did they still lose everything?") — the single most important early silence; it sets the hook.
- After "Knowing was never the problem." (end of Section 4) — the threshold into the film's core.
- After "It was speed." (end of Section 5) — let the thesis sit.
- After "Then Nokia jumped." (Section 6) — the gap *is* the drama.
- After "effectively, gone." (end of Section 6) — the death of the empire; hold it.
- Around the Law Card line (Section 7) — the longest, stillest silence in the episode. Near-silence before and after.
- After the final line (Section 9) — let it breathe before the end screen.

## Phrases That Must NOT Sound Like Hype

Deliver these flat, quiet, or grave — never punched, never trailer-voiced:

- "around forty percent" and "roughly four out of every ten phones" — plain, scoped, factual. Not a boast.
- "seven point two billion dollars" and "seven point six billion dollars" — delivered *quietly*; the scale carries itself. No exclamatory lift.
- "one of the most valuable and dominant mobile companies in the world" — grave respect, not a sales pitch.
- "one of the most studied gambles in modern business" — level and even, not sensational.
- "Then Nokia jumped." / "gone." — grave, not gleeful. Never mock the company.
- The Empire Law line — reverent restraint, not a motivational-speaker landing.
- The modern-warning examples — observational, not alarmist.

If any line starts to feel like a movie trailer or a hype reel, take the emotion *down* and re-read. Restraint is the brand.
